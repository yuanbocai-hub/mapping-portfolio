It traces these relationships through historical maps, multilingual place names, and travel accounts.

Project introduction

Reading a place
across sources

How did people travel through and govern the highlands of southeastern Tibet and northeastern India before modern international borders took shape? This historical GIS project brings together five batches of official Chinese place names, historical maps, multilingual gazetteers, and expedition accounts. Reading these sources together places a modern coordinate in a longer history. A named pass can be traced across spellings and map editions, located between watersheds, and connected to accounts of who crossed it and under what conditions.

I plotted the official coordinates in Google Earth, georeferenced 10 historical maps in QGIS, and digitized settlements, passes, monasteries, and district seats at their printed symbols. The gazetteer contains 1,582 place-name records across 13 datasets, including 220 mountain-pass records, 56 gompa or monastery records, and 41 dzong records. Each record represents an occurrence in a particular source. Keeping repeated names and uncertain matches allows comparison of how different mapmakers located and classified the same feature.

River valleys gave journeys a direction, but following a river did not guarantee passage. A gorge could force travellers uphill, while a pass could connect communities whose rivers flowed into different basins. Monasteries and pilgrimage institutions sustained movement through hospitality, shelter, and provisions. At Potrang, communities on opposite sides of the relay maintained separate rest houses and handed over responsibility for carrying official loads. Dzong, or district seats, organized movement through another set of relationships. District officers could mobilize food and porters, and their authority reached beyond the seat through subordinate officials along the valley.

Corridors before Borders reconstructs a spatial order that preceded modern boundaries. A pass joined valleys, a rest house marked where one community’s obligations gave way to another’s, and a dzong connected a river reach to a seat of authority. These relationships made the highlands a region people could navigate, provision, and govern. Comparing maps with travel accounts also makes the evidence behind that geography easier to examine: a mapped place may record a visit, a reported connection, or a cartographer’s interpretation. The project keeps those distinctions visible as it follows names and journeys across sources, without equating every mark with passage.

Historical background / Survey of India

The archive behind
the mapped region

Survey of India maps supply a cartographic framework for comparing the highlands across editions. The presentation places these sheets within a longer survey history, from the Great Trigonometrical Survey to Pundit journeys and the expeditions of Bailey, Morshead, and Kingdon Ward. Its timeline distinguishes the date of a journey from the later publication of its account.

For this project, a map is also evidence of how geographical knowledge took shape. Which names did a sheet retain? Which communities appeared under regional labels? Where did a mapped connection depend on testimony? Reading maps alongside expedition accounts allows me to examine these choices and the contributions of people whose knowledge entered the published record.

The early twentieth century is the focus of the historical interpretation. Later gazetteers, modern maps, and official coordinates provide comparison points for tracing how names and categories persisted or changed. Their inclusion does not make every record contemporary with the journeys.

Evidence / 13 core datasets

A gazetteer of
source occurrences

I retain each name as an occurrence in a particular source before considering whether it refers to the same place as another record. This preserves differences in spelling, location, and feature type. A repeated name can therefore become evidence for comparison rather than a duplicate removed during cleaning.

The 1,582 records come from four modern regional datasets, three Survey of India sheets, and six expedition datasets. The coverage map shows the extent of available digitized records. Its outlines do not represent complete map sheets or an exhaustive extraction of their contents.

Database design / Proposed SQL model

One place,
several histories of evidence

The working gazetteer motivates a relational design that separates a place from the names and positions assigned to it. A proposed PLACE record provides an identity to which multiple source-specific records can be linked. Each name attestation, location version, claim, or relationship retains its source.

This structure would let me compare disagreements without replacing them with a single coordinate or preferred spelling. It also makes interpretation reviewable: a claim that an actor visited a place can cite a passage, while a reported journey can retain the testimony through which it became known.

In the access-coding examples, visited requires evidence that a named actor reached a place; reported retains attributed information. not_mentioned describes the defined review scope and does not establish that a place was unvisited.

Two corridor studies

Crossing divides,
sustaining passage

The terrain maps connect named passes and settlements to specific passages in Bailey’s accounts. The comparison follows both the physical crossing and the arrangements that supplied travellers along the way.

At Yongyap La, the route crosses from the Dibang into the Tsangpo drainage. At Potrang, rest houses and carrying obligations mark a handover between communities. A continuous line on a map depends on these different kinds of connection.

Terrain and river context: Copernicus GLO-30 and HydroRIVERS; HydroBASINS supplies drainage units. Historical route positions are approximate. Similar name forms alone do not establish a route, a shared meaning, or a migration event.

Method & interpretation

A source record keeps
its own history

Names and positions

Each occurrence retains its source. Repeated spellings and alternative locations remain available for comparison. The proposed database separates place identity from name attestations, geometry versions, and source-backed assertions.

Observation and testimony

A traveller’s visit and a reported connection carry different kinds of evidence. The project distinguishes places reached by a named actor from routes known through testimony. An unmatched passage does not establish that a place was unvisited.

Knowledge relationships

Who made a place knowable?

Traders, residents, surveyors, and institutions enter the record through different encounters. The network follows documented relations between people, places, publications, and claims.
