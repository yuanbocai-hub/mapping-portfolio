## I. Principal findings

The book contains two different divisions into upper, middle, and lower reaches. They should not be conflated.

### 1. The modern geographical division in the translator’s preface

| Section | Extent | Geographical characteristics described in the preface |
|---|---|---|
| Upper reaches | Headwaters–Ichang, Hubei | Mainly crosses the first and second steps of China’s topography; numerous tributaries, a large elevation drop, and abundant hydropower resources |
| Middle reaches | Ichang–Hukou, Jiangxi | Mainly traverses plains; the Dongting Lake, Han River, and Poyang Lake systems join, substantially increasing discharge |
| Lower reaches | Hukou–Shanghai | Plains with fewer tributaries, low relief, and still greater discharge |

Evidence: translator’s preface, p. 1; combined PDF, p. 4. This is compiler-translator Wang Yuhe’s overview of the Yangtze’s physical geography, rather than the navigation divisions used in Worcester’s main text.

- Chapter 24, book p. 761; combined PDF p. 788: the upper-river vessel inventory begins with the mayangzi junk.

## II. Why Worcester used this division

Worcester’s division is primarily a **classification of navigation and vessel types**, rather than the river-section scheme used in present-day hydrology or physical-geography textbooks.

Four considerations are central:

1. **Changes in navigational conditions.** Below Hankow, navigation primarily uses broad, low-gradient channels. Hankow–Ichang remains largely a river of plains, meanders, shoals, and embankments. Above Ichang, the channel narrows markedly and gorges, swift currents, and dangerous rapids become frequent.
2. **Changes in vessel types and handling techniques.** Lower-river craft include deep-draft cargo vessels, rice boats, salt boats, harbor craft, and delta sampans. Middle-river types provide a transition toward shallow-water navigation, poling, and some rapid-running conditions. Upper-river vessels emphasize shallow draft, strong hulls, balanced rudders, long sweeps, tracking, and adaptation to rapids.
3. **Cargo transshipment at hub ports.** Ichang is the clearest break. The book explicitly describes vessels arriving from the more navigable middle and lower reaches, unloading cargo, and transferring it to craft specially built for upper-river rapids.
4. **Junk guilds, home ports, and regular trading routes.** Worcester often classifies vessels by where they were built, their home ports, and their normal routes. A type may cross two sections, so these boundaries are not absolute natural divisions. For example, shaomayang and Jing-bang craft operate between Hankow and Ichang and may also reach Chungking. Mayangzi originated in the Hunan river system and later became a representative Sichuan gorge-river type.

Accordingly, “associated waterways” in the following inventory means the **principal construction areas, home ports, or regular operating waters recorded by Worcester**. It does not mean that a vessel could appear on only one river.

## IV. Points to bear in mind when using the inventory

1. **Names often combine place, use, and appearance.** “Nanking cargo junk” combines location and function; “rice boat” and “salt boat” identify commodities; “crooked-stern junk” and “red embroidered shoe boat” are vernacular names based on appearance; “mayangzi” and “Jing-bang junk” refer to origins or guild affiliations.
2. **Identical names need not identify identical types.** “Sand boat,” for example, occurs in both the estuary and Min River chapters. Waters, dimensions, construction, and rig must be considered; names alone do not justify merging entries.
3. **Types spread and undergo modification.** Mayangzi originated in Hunan’s Yuan River system and later entered Sichuan, adapting to the gorges above Ichang. A name’s place of origin, a vessel’s building location, and its actual operating area may therefore differ.
4. **Worcester’s “Yangtze basin” is a navigation-network concept.** Besides the main stem, his vessel survey includes Taihu, the Grand Canal, Poyang Lake–Gan River, Dongting Lake–Xiang/Zi/Yuan rivers, the Han River, and Sichuan tributaries. This is a waterway network organized around ports, trade, and vessel diffusion, rather than modern watershed-management boundaries.

## V. The simplest distinction

For physical geography, use the modern division in the translator’s preface, with **Ichang and Hukou** as the two boundaries.

For Worcester’s organization of vessel types, guilds, and routes, use the main text’s navigation division, with **Hankow and Ichang** as the boundaries:

> Estuary/Shanghai–Hankow: lower reaches; Hankow–Ichang: middle reaches; above Ichang: upper reaches.

This division rests on navigational conditions, vessel adaptation, cargo transshipment, and traditional trading routes, rather than a single geomorphological criterion.

### 3. Differences in port structure across the three sections

- **Lower river:** large markets, lake gateways, and main-stem/tributary distribution predominate. Shanghai–Nanking–Wuhu–Kiukiang–Hankow forms the main-stem backbone; Zhenjiang, Yueh-chow, and Changde connect canal and lake networks to it.
- **Middle river:** Shasi’s intersecting waterways and Ichang’s upper/lower vessel transfers stand out; Xindi and Yidu connect local networks.
- **Upper river:** ports resemble a chain of staged transfer points shaped by rapids. Chungking is the overall market; Fow-Chow, Kungt’an, Luzhou, Kiating, and Dengjingguan control tributary entrances, navigation endpoints, or compulsory vessel changes.
