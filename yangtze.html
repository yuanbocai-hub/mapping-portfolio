The Yangtze
as a river transportation system

A cargo could travel much farther than the boat that carried it. In his 1938 account of Yangtze trade, Joseph Earle Spencer describes large junks taking goods back to their home ports, where crews loaded them onto smaller boats for the shallower tributaries.

I redrew seven commodities from a 1935 map to see where their production areas lay along the Yangtze and its tributaries. Cotton led me to Shasi, a port in the middle-river plains where cotton from the surrounding district entered the export trade. For a closer look at the boats, I turned to G. R. G. Worcester, a river inspector with the Chinese Maritime Customs Service. At Fuling in Sichuan, he records salt cargoes changing boats for the journey up the Wu River. The vessel that took them upriver was the crooked-stern junk I set out to model.

Tracing a hemp boundary through the Chengdu sugar district

形势 Xíngshì

I first understood this word by looking at tributaries: a valley directs water one way, a ridge sends it another. The commodity sheet made me read the same terrain differently. Around Chengdu, I wanted to be able to follow a river through country marked for both sugar and hemp.

With heavier fills I kept losing the river. I lightened the tints and interrupted some bands in the final drawing. The underlying layers keep the overlap, including the stretches where I left an edge off the printed map.

The Yangtze

02 / Reconstruction

Separating the ink,
interpreting the region

At first glance, the source looks like a mass of color. Several commodities occupy the same space, but the map also mixes filled patches, patterned areas, and colored outlines. Fading and overprinting make those marks harder to separate. A single color threshold cannot reliably tell a commodity from a river, a railway, or another product.

  1. 01

    Anchor the main map

    I used the printed graticule to georeference the main sheets and excluded the legends and separate insets from the commodity extraction. A constrained quadratic transformation accommodated scan distortion. Independent checks helped distinguish a good fit to the grid from the much looser geography of the historical map.

  2. 02

    Classify marks in context

    Local samples guided color and texture extraction. Reviewed windows helped separate similar marks: red sugar patches from railways, dark purple tea from pale hemp outlines, and tobacco texture from water tint. I checked each commodity against the scan rather than treating every matching pixel as evidence.

  3. 03

    Return to the source

    I checked the broken outlines against the scan and saved inferred boundary segments separately so I could revisit the joins.

Yangtze / interpreted regions

Reconstructing an enclosed area

Filled symbols became regions; interrupted rings were interpreted as enclosing areas. Where ink was missing, visible contours and reviewed outlines guided the reconstruction. Inferred boundary segments were recorded separately. I counted 91 reconstructed features across seven classes. That count reflects decisions such as joining the rice outline across Hubei and Hunan.

Pearl River / retained marks

Keeping the gaps in the band

Rice, sugar, silk, and tussah silk retain the width and interruptions of the printed bands. Tea and tobacco remain patches. The export contains 188 disconnected fragments across six classes; those fragments are pieces of cartographic ink, not a count of agricultural regions.

Accuracy and display decisions

The Yangtze grid check gave a residual of about 7.45 source pixels, or 7.1 km. This measures the registration check, not absolute historical accuracy. On the Pearl River sheet, the final internal graticule residual was about 3.2 pixels; comparison with 13 historical city symbols gave an RMSE of about 20.7 km. Those checks measure different things.

The final Yangtze layout hides selected hemp segments where they compete with other symbols. The source GeoJSON retains the overlap. A missing segment in the presentation therefore does not establish an absence of production. Neither dataset supports estimates of actual cultivated area. Here, “natural gaps” means breaks in the printed bands; no Jenks classification was used in this extraction.

Crooked-stern junk model project

Where did a cargo have to change boats?

The 1935 sheet gives the products of the basin a place on the map. For the work of moving a cargo, I turned to G. R. G. Worcester’s account of the Wu River. At Fow-Chow (Fuling), freight could pass between the tributary and the Yangtze. Upstream at Kung-t’an, a shipment had to change boats. Four more stages led toward Ssu-nan.

I drew this chart as an experiment with Codex, which helped me plot the river and lay out the stages. Worcester gives a total of 71 difficult rapids for the reach from Kung-t’an to Ssu-nan, so I marked the reach as a whole.

Reading the chart. Hsin-t’an is interpolated from historical stage distances. Non-uniform scaling gives the Wu River more room on the page.

Building the crooked-stern junk 歪屁股船

At YUE-FAN Studio we wanted to know why a Wu River salt boat had one side of its stern built higher than the other. Worcester’s drawings gave us the hull lines. His account of the long steering sweeps helped us work out what that uneven stern allowed the crew to do on the water.

We built a digital model, then worked out how to assemble the hull and its fittings as a wooden kit. Shipwright Zhang Yuqi advised us as we revised the design through eight versions. The raised stern gives one sweep a fulcrum and leaves room for the other to swing.

The Science Museum Group also holds a historical model of this type, built by Chinese shipwrights under Worcester’s supervision. View the museum record ↗

From the route to the vessel

Pulling upstream.
Keeping control downstream.

A route line says little about the work of making a passage. These annotated plates bring that work back into view: where a towing line meets the hull, how it clears the bow, and how long sweeps are operated.

Against the current

Following the pull of a line

The upstream diagram follows force from the shore to the boat. Bamboo towing lines attach to the tracking beams. A lifting line and iron ring allow the towing line to pass over the raised bow when it changes sides.

A separate shore line controls the boat’s distance from the bank. The arrangement makes the riverbank part of the transport system: the crew’s work aboard depends on the people and attachment points ashore.

With the current

Steering takes centre stage

The second plate shows how the boat’s steering equipment was arranged. Long sweeps reach beyond the hull, and an elevated platform gives the stern operator room to work. The raised side of the stern provides a fulcrum and helps keep the sweeps apart.

I place this image beside the route chart because the shape of the boat matters at the scale of a river reach. The image documents the equipment and working positions; it does not establish the conditions of a particular rapid descent.

Comparative extension / a second river region

The Pearl River and eastern Kuang-tung

In the south I wanted to know how far the economic region around Canton extended upriver. Hong’s sheet follows production beyond the watershed, which matters when the question is where a market draws its goods. Its six commodity categories follow a different legend: tussah silk, tobacco, sugar, silk, tea, and rice. The main sheet also extends beyond the drainage basin. I retained that coverage rather than cutting the historical evidence to a modern watershed boundary.
