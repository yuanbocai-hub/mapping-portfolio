The Yangtze
as a river transportation system

I redrew seven commodity distributions from Hong Maoxi’s 1935 map to show where goods were produced along the Yangtze and its tributaries. The map gives the crooked-stern junk a wider setting, placing its home waters on the Wu River within a trading region that stretched from Sichuan to the coast.

A cargo could travel much farther than the boat that carried it.

In his 1938 account of Yangtze trade, Joseph Earle Spencer describes large junks taking goods back to their home ports, where crews loaded them onto smaller boats for the shallower tributaries.

J. E. Spencer · Trade and Transshipment in the Yangtze Valley · 1938

Reading the Yangtze through names and navigation

Historical accounts led me to ask how river names, regional identities, and navigation described different geographies of the same river. These offered ways of understanding the Yangtze beyond the Strahler and Shreve systems I had learned in hydrology.

Names that change in scope

Yangtze began as a local toponym on the lower course, from Nanking to the estuary. English-language accounts later applied it much farther upstream in the nineteenth century.

A river of regional names

Chin-sha Chiang (the River of Golden Sand), Ch’uan Chiang in Szechuan, Ching Chiang, and Wan Chiang give particular stretches their own regional identities. Descriptions of the gorge reaches add another way of recognising the river through its landscape.

Reaches connected by cargo

Different boats.
Connected journeys.

Currents, rapids, vessel design, handling practices, boatmen’s affiliations, home ports, and trading relationships distinguished one reach from another. At Ichang, transshipment connected upper-river craft with vessels serving the middle and lower Yangtze and its lake networks.

Following cargo from the Wu Chiang through Fu-chou reveals how a boat built for one reach participated in a wider river network.

Cartographic choices

Placing the boat in its historical geography

To place the crooked-stern junk within the historical geography of the Yangtze, I brought together administrative boundaries, historical place names, and the river network.

To reconstruct the administrative setting, I used the 1911 administrative boundary shapefiles published by the China Historical GIS (CHGIS) project as a late Qing baseline. I labelled administrative seats with their late Qing names in Wade–Giles, adding modern names in parentheses where they differ. This provides a historical reference for reading the later Republican-era sources.

I gave particular visual emphasis to the Yangtze’s major tributaries and their tributaries in Szechuan and the upper basin, making it easier to follow their connections to the middle-river lakes and plains and onward to the estuary. Read alongside Republican-era maps and publications of the Chinese Maritime Customs Service, this river network provides a geographical framework for tracing routes and transfers described in the sources.

Tracing a hemp boundary through the Chengdu sugar district

形势 Xíngshì

I first understood this word by looking at tributaries: a valley directs water one way, a ridge sends it another. The commodity sheet made me read the same terrain differently. Around Chengdu, I wanted to be able to follow a river through country marked for both sugar and hemp.

The pale, broken bands form loose rings, some of them left open. They indicate areas of commodity production within their outlines; production is not confined to the bands themselves.

With heavier fills I kept losing both the river and the shapes of the commodity regions. I lightened the tints and opened selected edges to make their spatial patterns easier to see: where production areas cluster, how they extend across the basin, and where they overlap. I wanted readers to follow the shape and orientation of these regions while keeping the river network in view. The underlying layers retain the overlaps, including stretches where I omitted an edge in the displayed map.

02 / Reconstruction Separating the ink, interpreting the region

At first glance, the source looks like a mass of color. Several commodities occupy the same space, but the map also mixes filled patches, patterned areas, and colored outlines. Fading and overprinting make those marks harder to separate. A single color threshold cannot reliably tell a commodity from a river, a railway, or another product.

  1. 01

    Anchor the main map

    I used the printed graticule to georeference the main sheets and excluded the legends and separate insets from the commodity extraction. A constrained quadratic transformation accommodated scan distortion. Independent checks helped distinguish a good fit to the grid from the much looser geography of the historical map.

  2. 02

    Classify marks in context

    Local samples guided color and texture extraction. Reviewed windows helped separate similar marks: red sugar patches from railways, dark purple tea from pale hemp outlines, and tobacco texture from water tint. I checked each commodity against the scan rather than treating every matching pixel as evidence.

  3. 03

    Return to the source

    I checked the broken outlines against the scan and saved inferred boundary segments separately so I could revisit the joins.

How the two maps differ

Yangtze / interpreted regions

Reconstructing an enclosed area

Filled symbols became regions; interrupted rings were interpreted as enclosing areas. Where ink was missing, visible contours and reviewed outlines guided the reconstruction. Inferred boundary segments were recorded separately. I counted 91 reconstructed features across seven classes. That count reflects decisions such as joining the rice outline across Hubei and Hunan.

Pearl River / retained marks

Keeping the gaps in the band

Rice, sugar, silk, and tussah silk retain the width and interruptions of the printed bands. Tea and tobacco remain patches. The export contains 188 disconnected fragments across six classes; those fragments are pieces of cartographic ink, not a count of agricultural regions.

Accuracy and display decisions

The Yangtze grid check gave a residual of about 7.45 source pixels, or 7.1 km. This measures the registration check, not absolute historical accuracy. On the Pearl River sheet, the final internal graticule residual was about 3.2 pixels; comparison with 13 historical city symbols gave an RMSE of about 20.7 km. Those checks measure different things.

The final Yangtze layout hides selected hemp segments where they compete with other symbols. The source GeoJSON retains the overlap. A missing segment in the presentation therefore does not establish an absence of production. Neither dataset supports estimates of actual cultivated area. Here, “natural gaps” means breaks in the printed bands; no Jenks classification was used in this extraction.

Crooked-stern junk model project

Where did a cargo have to change boats?

The 1935 sheet gives the products of the basin a place on the map. For the work of moving a cargo, I turned to G. R. G. Worcester’s account of the Wu River. At Fow-Chow (Fuling), freight could pass between the tributary and the Yangtze. Upstream at Kung-t’an, a shipment had to change boats. Four more stages led toward Ssu-nan.

I drew this chart as an experiment with Codex to explore a different visual approach, using non-uniform scaling to give the Wu River more space while keeping its connection to the Yangtze visible. This makes the Wu River’s navigation stages easier to follow, while showing how the steamboat route from Ichang to Chungking passed its mouth at Fow-Chow—present-day Fuling (涪陵).

Worcester gives a total of 71 difficult rapids for the reach from Kung-t’an to Ssu-nan, so I marked the reach as a whole.

Reading the chart. Hsin-t’an is interpolated from historical stage distances. Non-uniform scaling gives the Wu River more room on the page.

The photograph makes the boats’ working equipment visible: long sweeps extend beyond the hulls, ropes cross between vessels and shore, and woven canopies cover the living and cargo spaces.

Read alongside Worcester’s drawings and descriptions, images like this helped us ask how the vessel’s structure supported the work of moving and steering it.

Building the crooked-stern junk 歪屁股船

At YUE-FAN Studio we wanted to know why a Wu River salt boat had one side of its stern built higher than the other. Worcester’s drawings gave us the hull lines. His account of the long steering sweeps helped us work out what that uneven stern allowed the crew to do on the water.

We built a digital model, then worked out how to assemble the hull and its fittings as a wooden kit. Shipwright Zhang Yuqi advised us as we revised the design through eight versions. The raised stern gives one sweep a fulcrum and leaves room for the other to swing.

A route line says little about the work of making a passage. These annotated plates bring that work back into view: where a towing line meets the hull, how it clears the bow, and how long sweeps are operated.

Against the current

Following the pull of a line

The upstream diagram follows force from the shore to the boat. Bamboo towing lines attach to the tracking beams. A lifting line and iron ring allow the towing line to pass over the raised bow when it changes sides.

A separate shore line controls the boat’s distance from the bank. The arrangement makes the riverbank part of the transport system: the crew’s work aboard depends on the people and attachment points ashore.

Comparative extension / a second river region

The Pearl River and eastern Kuang-tung

I wanted to know how far Canton’s economic region extended upriver, and how these connections shaped regional inequality. Historically, rice and other primary products from Kwang-Hsi helped sustain the commercial economy of Canton and the surrounding region. I ask whether today’s labour migration from Kwang-Hsi to coastal factories echoes that earlier pattern—and how the outflow affects the region’s demographic dividend.

Hong’s sheet maps six commodities—tussah silk, tobacco, sugar, silk, tea, and rice. I retained its coverage beyond the drainage basin to follow the historical geography of supply rather than impose a modern watershed boundary.

From the map to the river

Following my home river upstream

Photography essayEncountering the Many Embodiments of the West River

Growing up in Shenzhen, Guangdong, beside Hong Kong and near the Pearl River estuary, I knew the river first through life at its downstream end. Shenzhen’s rapid growth during the reform era was the backdrop to my childhood. Learning to see the wider basin has become a way of getting to know the river I call home.

Behind the skyline are connections that are less visible in accounts of urban growth: workers arriving from Kwang-Hsi for coastal factories, river sand carried downstream for construction, electricity generated at upstream hydropower stations, and water supplied through reservoirs and transfer systems. Mapping the Pearl River became part of my effort to understand how everyday life near the estuary depends on people, materials, and waterways farther inland.

My photographic essay follows this inquiry along the river, through encounters with fishers, boat crews, and riverside residents.
