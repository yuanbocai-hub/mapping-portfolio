# Five Functional Categories of Yangtze Ports and Cities in Worcester’s Collected Chinese Edition: Classification and Textual Evidence

*Translation note: This document translates the supplied Chinese research note on《中国长江帆船通鉴》. Quoted passages below are translated from the Chinese edition as cited in that note; they are not presented as verified quotations from Worcester’s original English. Book and combined-PDF page references are preserved. Familiar historical English place names are used where appropriate, with Chinese names retained at first identification where useful.*

## I. Purpose and scope

This document classifies the principal ports, cities, and route nodes appearing in the book by their actual functions within the water-transport network.

The classification is a research synthesis based on Worcester’s descriptions of channels, vessel types, commodity flows, tributary systems, and changes of boat. **It is not a city-ranking system explicitly proposed by Worcester himself.**

Its principal coverage comprises:

- the lower river: the Yangtze estuary and Shanghai–Hankow, together with Taihu, the Grand Canal, Poyang Lake, Han River, Dongting Lake, and the Xiang, Zi, and Yuan river networks;
- the middle river: Hankow–Ichang as defined by Worcester;
- the upper river: above Ichang, Chungking, and the Sichuan tributary networks;
- places already identified in the present notes as trading ports, tributary gateways, vessel-transfer ports, or ordinary route nodes.

It is not an indiscriminate list of every place in the book. Temples, villages, peaks, sandbars, and points used solely for channel surveying are included only where they help explain an ordinary route node.

### 3. Strength of evidence

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Direct** | The text explicitly uses terms such as trade center, transshipment port, head of navigation, or transshipment center |
| **Functionally clear** | No explicit label is given, but commodity flows, regular routes, vessel changes, or market reach sufficiently support the classification |
| **General mention** | Mainly described as a town, village, stopping place, boatbuilding location, or navigation landmark, without evidence of an interregional port function |

## III. Five port functions

### A. Ordinary river-route node

#### Definition

A city or settlement serves mainly as a navigation landmark, local landing, ordinary stopping place, supply point, boatbuilding location, or short-distance route node. The text provides no evidence that it controls a large market, brings together several interregional routes, or performs a regular vessel-transfer function.

“Ordinary” refers to its **relatively general function in the navigation network depicted by the book**, not its administrative rank, population, or historical importance.

#### Criteria

A place may be classified as A if one of the following applies and strong evidence for B–D is absent:

1. It is simply the next town or village along a route.
2. Only local stopping, ferrying, boatbuilding, or short-distance transport is recorded.
3. It is historically important, but the text assigns it no important trade or transshipment function.
4. The available material is insufficient to support another category.

#### Examples from the book

- Jiayu (嘉鱼) is described as “Jiayu village” on red-earth cliffs on the Yangtze’s right bank. It functions principally as a navigation landmark, without an assigned interregional port role. Book p. 555; combined PDF p. 574.
- Around Yanglingji (阳陵矶), the text mentions “nothing worth noting apart from one or two cliffs.” Book p. 555; combined PDF p. 574.
- Jiangling/Jingzhou (江陵／荆州) has historical and military importance, but Chapter 23 emphasizes that history rather than a contemporary transshipment function. Book p. 557; combined PDF p. 576.

### B. Regional commercial-network center

#### Definition

A port has a substantial market and hinterland, gathers multiple commodities, connects several interregional routes, or is explicitly called an important trade center, distribution center, or market by Worcester.

Its importance lies in organizing a regional commercial network, not simply in occupying a river confluence.

#### Criteria

Usually at least two of the following apply:

1. The text explicitly calls it a trade center, distribution center, or important market.
2. Goods originate in several prefectures, counties, provinces, or tributary systems.
3. It handles diverse commodities and redistributes them.
4. It is a common origin or destination for several long-distance routes.
5. Vessel numbers, freight volumes, or market scale clearly exceed those of a local port.

#### Examples from the book

- Shanghai: “In practice, all goods and commodities entering and leaving Shanghai travel by water.” Book p. 147; combined PDF p. 160.
- Zhenhai (镇海): “Zhenhai, the gateway to the Yong River, is also an important trade center.” Book p. 118; combined PDF p. 131.
- Xindi (新堤): “On the Yangtze’s right bank, 101 miles above Hankow, lies the fairly important trade center of Xindi.” Book p. 555; combined PDF p. 574.
- Shasi (沙市): important “not only because it is a cotton-producing place and transshipment port, but also because it lies at the center of a network of canals and waterways.” Book p. 556; combined PDF p. 575.
- Yiyang (益阳): “a large timber-distribution center … the coal trade is also quite prosperous.” Book p. 528; combined PDF p. 547.
- Kiating (嘉定): “Kiating is the head of navigation for large sailing vessels on the Min River and has therefore become an important trade and transshipment center.” Book p. 812; combined PDF p. 839.

### C. Tributary trade-route junction

#### Definition

A port occupies an interface between a main stem and tributary, a lake or canal entrance, several tributaries, or different waterway networks. It allows goods from tributary hinterlands to enter the Yangtze trunk route or Yangtze cargo to enter inland networks.

This category emphasizes **topological position in the waterway network**. It is not synonymous with B: some tributary entrances are gateways with small markets, while some large markets are not at tributary confluences.

#### Criteria

1. It lies where a tributary joins a main stem.
2. It lies where a lake or canal network enters the Yangtze.
3. Several navigable tributaries meet nearby.
4. The text explains that tributary produce enters the Yangtze or another principal route at the port.
5. It connects routes across basins or waterway networks.

#### Examples from the book

- Yueh-chow (岳州): “Dongting Lake is connected with the Yangtze by a short waterway at Yueh-chow … [which] also becomes an especially active center of vessel traffic.” Book p. 506; combined PDF p. 525.
- Yidu (宜都): “A tributary called the Qing River … enters the Yangtze at the strongly walled city of Yidu. Trade routes from southern Sichuan also join the Yangtze here.” Book p. 557; combined PDF p. 576.
- Hezhou (合州): the Suining, Baoning, and Qu rivers “all join the Chia-ling at … Hezhou or nearby.” Book p. 790; combined PDF p. 817.
- Hejiang (合江): the Chishui enters the Yangtze nearby. Timber rafts are regrouped at its mouth before descending the Yangtze to Chungking. Book p. 805; combined PDF p. 832.
- Kiating: the Chengdu Plain’s waterways converge at Jiangkou, and the Min receives the Tong and Ya rivers at Kiating. Book p. 812; combined PDF p. 839.

### D. Vessel-type and cargo transshipment port between upper and lower routes

#### Definition

Differences in upper- and lower-route navigational conditions, vessel draft, hull construction, propulsion, or shipping organization require cargo to be unloaded, lightened, transferred to another vessel, or shifted to a different transport mode at a particular port.

This category most directly reflects Worcester’s understanding of Yangtze shipping through the relationship **channel conditions → vessel adaptation → cargo transfer**.

#### Two mechanisms

| Subtype | Name | Criteria | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **D-S** | Sectional/institutionalized transshipment | At the boundary between two navigation sections, vessel systems, or draft classes; vessel changes are established and routine | Ichang, Changde, Dengjingguan |
| **D-T** | Technical/compulsory transshipment | A particular rapid, dangerous reach, shoal, dam, or interruption of continuous navigation forces unloading, lightening, or a change of boat | Kungt’an, upstream of Yonghuaiting, Laoyatan |

“Technical transshipment port” is therefore best treated as a subtype of D, rather than separated entirely from upper/lower vessel and cargo transfer. If a map needs to emphasize technical navigation constraints, D-T may receive its own symbol.

#### Examples from the book

- Ichang: “Vessels arriving from the more navigable middle and lower Yangtze transfer their cargo here to other vessels specially built for the upper Yangtze’s swift currents and dangerous rapids.” Book p. 558; combined PDF p. 577.
- Ichang is also explicitly called “the transshipment center for upper-Yangtze routes.” Book p. 559; combined PDF p. 578.
- Changde (常德): “When cargo has to pass from deep-draft vessels to shallow-draft vessels, Changde becomes an important transshipment port.” Book p. 539; combined PDF p. 558.
- Kungt’anho: transport “must be divided into five stages, with cargo repeatedly changing boats”; Kungt’an is “the first obstacle to continuous navigation.” Book p. 843; combined PDF p. 870.
- Upstream of Yonghuaiting (永怀亭): cargo “must be unloaded to pass … the difficult Pintan Rapids.” Book p. 808; combined PDF p. 835.
- Dengjingguan (邓井关): “Salt is transferred here to upper-Yangtze craft, usually zhongyuan lighters or willow-leaf boats.” Book p. 840; combined PDF p. 867.
- Tangjiatuo (唐家沱): salt is “sold locally or transferred to larger sailing vessels.” Book p. 840; combined PDF p. 867.

### E. Specialized commodity-shipping port

#### Definition

A port’s shipping role centers on one or a few characteristic commodities, such as salt, porcelain, timber, coal, rice, or cotton. It may be a production-site loading port, a collection or processing center, or a gateway for outward shipment.

Category E describes **commodity specialization**, not market scale. A specialized commodity port can be a large regional center or a small loading point.

#### Criteria

1. The text repeatedly links the place directly to a commodity.
2. Dedicated vessel types are designed or named for that commodity.
3. The port is a key production, collection, loading, or outward-shipping node.
4. The commodity clearly shapes local vessels, navigation seasons, or transfer arrangements.

#### Examples from the book

- Jingdezhen (景德镇): called the “porcelain capital,” with finished products “shipped to the outside world through Kiukiang.” Book p. 488; combined PDF p. 507.
- Yiyang: “a large timber-distribution center,” also with dedicated vessels carrying coal to Hankow. Book p. 528; combined PDF p. 547.
- Hongjiang (洪江): “the principal transshipment port for timber from western Hunan and northern Guizhou.” Book p. 548; combined PDF p. 567.
- Hejiang: downstream Chishui traffic mainly carries timber, charcoal, and bamboo; timber is assembled at the mouth for shipment to Chungking. Book p. 805; combined PDF p. 832.
- Ziliujing (自流井): Chapter 33 concerns well salt, crooked-headed salt boats, and Yanjing River transport; low water also requires unloading and vessel changes at Laoyatan. Book pp. 829–841; combined PDF pp. 856–868.
- Wuhu: “Wuhu is the home of cargo boats,” with concentrations of rice boats, salt boats, and Anhui inland cargo craft. Book p. 444; combined PDF p. 463.

## IV. Functions, not a rigid urban hierarchy

These five categories should not be read as administrative ranks from low to high. A more useful conceptual sequence is:

```text
Basic route function: A Ordinary river-route node
        ↓
Specialization or network access: C Tributary junction / E Commodity port
        ↓
Regional organization: B Regional commercial-network center
        ↓
Control over the navigation system: D Vessel and cargo transshipment port
```

The sequence indicates functional complexity, not a fixed ranking:

- a D port can be important because of rapids while having a small market, as at Kungt’an;
- a B city can be a large market without routine vessel changes, as at Shanghai;
- an E port can be a large industrial city or a single-commodity loading point;
- one city may combine two or three of B, C, D, and E.

For databases and maps, the recommended structure is therefore:

> **Primary type + secondary functions + transfer mechanism + evidence strength**

Do not force every city into a single exclusive category.

### Lower-river structure

The principal pattern is:

> Large markets + lake/canal gateways + specialized outward commodity shipping

Shanghai–Nanking–Wuhu–Kiukiang–Hankow forms the main-stem market backbone. Zhenjiang, Yueh-chow, and Changde connect canal and lake systems. Jingdezhen, Yiyang, and Hongjiang support specialized shipping in porcelain, timber, and coal.

### Three principal middle-river functions

1. **Shasi: B + C.** Regional commercial-network center and intersection of canals, waterways, and interregional routes.
2. **Yidu: C.** Tributary gateway where the Qing River and traditional southern-Sichuan routes join the Yangtze.
3. **Ichang: D-S.** The clearest institutionalized transfer port between ordinary middle/lower craft and upper-river rapid craft.

Although Xindi is explicitly called an important trade center, its hinterland mainly lies around Huanggai Lake. It should therefore be understood as a **local commercial center**, below Shasi and Hankow in scale and network reach.

### Upper-river structure

Three factors jointly determine the importance of upper-river ports:

1. **Tributary entrances:** Hezhou, Jiangjin, Fow-Chow, Hejiang, Luzhou, Xufu, Kiating.
2. **Changes in vessel type and operating waters:** Chungking, Fow-Chow, Jintang, Xufu, Kiating, Dengjingguan, Tangjiatuo.
3. **Compulsory staged transport caused by rapids:** Kungt’an, upstream of Yonghuaiting, Laoyatan on the Yanjing River.

Chungking is the overall market toward which many tributary cargoes converge. Fow-Chow, Kungt’an, Kiating, Dengjingguan, and others control tributary entrances, heads of junk navigation, or compulsory vessel changes.

## IX. Interpretations that are easily confused

### 1. Ichang is not an ordinary regional commercial center

Worcester explicitly states that Ichang has “no local commerce or products worth mentioning” and that its importance “arises solely from its geographical position.” Its most accurate primary category is therefore D-S, rather than B.

Evidence: book p. 558; combined PDF p. 577.

### 2. Shasi and Ichang derive their importance from different sources

- Shasi’s importance comes from cotton, transshipment, markets, and intersecting waterways: B + C.
- Ichang’s importance comes from structural transfers between middle/lower craft and upper-river rapid craft: D-S.

Both matter, but they are different kinds of port.

### 3. Kungt’an is not a key port because of market size

Its importance mainly comes from rapids interrupting continuous navigation. It is a typical D-T port, not a typical B center.

### 4. Technical transfer need not occur at an administrative city center

The Yonghuaiting transfer center lies upstream of the town. The technical bottleneck for Jingdezhen porcelain transport lies downstream in the Hongjiang reach. Compulsory transfer on the Yanjing occurs at the Laoyatan dam. Map symbols should therefore identify the actual obstruction or transfer point rather than invariably occupy a city-center coordinate.

### 5. Commodity specialization and regional commercial centrality can overlap

- Ziliujing is both a large salt-production/loading center and a regional market.
- Yiyang is both a specialized timber port and a Zi River distribution center.
- Hongjiang is both a timber port and a transshipment port.
- Shasi is both a cotton port and an interregional waterway-network center.

Suggested map symbols:

- use a large base symbol for the primary type;
- overlay smaller symbols for secondary functions;
- distinguish D-S and D-T through different centers or outlines;
- add a commodity abbreviation or pictogram for E, such as salt, timber, porcelain, or cotton;
- use dashed outlines for inferred classifications with insufficient evidence.

## XI. Final conclusion

Worcester’s account suggests that Yangtze ports should not be ordered solely by city size. They should be understood through four linked considerations:

1. position in the waterway network;
2. markets and hinterlands controlled;
3. specialized commodities shipped;
4. whether channel conditions force vessels and cargo to change here.

The five functional categories can therefore be defined as:

> **A ordinary river-route node; B regional commercial-network center; C tributary trade-route junction; D upper/lower vessel-type and cargo transshipment port; E specialized commodity-shipping port.**

Category D is subdivided into:

> **D-S sectional/institutionalized transshipment; D-T technical/compulsory transshipment.**

This retains both the notes’ specialized commodity ports and the technically constrained transfer ports that are particularly important for mapping, without conflating two different analytical dimensions.
